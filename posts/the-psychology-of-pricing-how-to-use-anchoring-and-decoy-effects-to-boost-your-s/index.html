The Psychology of Pricing: How to Use Anchoring and Decoy Effects to Boost Your Shopify Sales

2026-06-21 · Marketing · Tags: The, ecommerceempire

Pricing is more than just a number on a product page—it's a psychological trigger that can make or break your ecommerce success. While most Shopify store owners focus on product selection, marketing channels, and conversion optimization, they often overlook the subtle art of pricing strategy. Yet, the way you present prices can dramatically influence how customers perceive value, compare options, and ultimately decide to buy. In this article, we'll dive deep into two powerful psychological principles—anchoring and the decoy effect—and show you exactly how to apply them to your Shopify store to increase average order value, boost conversion rates, and maximize revenue without slashing your prices.

Understanding Anchoring: The First Number Sets the Stage

Anchoring is a cognitive bias where individuals rely heavily on the first piece of information they encounter when making decisions. In pricing, the first price a customer sees becomes the "anchor" against which all subsequent prices are judged. For example, if you show a high-priced item first, a moderately priced item suddenly seems like a bargain. This isn't just a theory—it's a well-documented phenomenon in behavioral economics, and it's used everywhere from restaurant menus to luxury car dealerships.

In your Shopify store, anchoring can be applied in several ways. The most straightforward method is to display a higher-priced product or bundle before showing your target product. For instance, if you sell a premium coffee maker at $299, you might first show a commercial-grade model at $499. The $299 price now appears more reasonable, even if it's still a significant investment. Alternatively, you can use a "compare at" price feature—Shopify allows you to set a compare-at price that appears struck through, creating an anchor that makes your actual price look like a steal. This is a classic tactic, but many store owners underutilize it by setting unrealistic compare-at prices. The key is to make the anchor believable—if you claim a $200 product was originally $500, customers may become skeptical. Instead, use a compare-at price that reflects a genuine market value or a previous price you've actually charged.

Another powerful anchoring technique is to present a high-priced "premium" option first, even if you don't expect many sales from it. This is often called the "goldilocks" strategy. By offering a luxury version, a standard version, and a budget version, you anchor customers to the high price, making the standard version seem like the smart middle ground. We'll explore this more in the next section, but the core idea is that the first price you show sets the tone for everything else.

The Decoy Effect: How to Steer Customers Toward Your Preferred Option

The decoy effect, also known as the asymmetric dominance effect, occurs when consumers change their preference between two options when a third, strategically designed option is introduced. The decoy is intentionally made to be less attractive than one of the original options, but it's not meant to be chosen—it's there to make the target option look better. This is a classic pricing strategy used by movie theaters (small, medium, large popcorn) and subscription services (basic, standard, premium).

Let's say you sell a digital course on Shopify. You have two main packages: a basic version at $49 and a premium version at $99. Most customers might choose the basic, leaving you with lower revenue. Now, introduce a third option: a "deluxe" version at $149 that includes everything in the premium plus extra coaching calls. The deluxe is the decoy—it's overpriced and not meant to sell. But its presence makes the $99 premium look like a fantastic deal in comparison. Customers who were on the fence between $49 and $99 will now gravitate toward the $99 because it seems like a bargain relative to the $149 decoy. The decoy doesn't have to be a real product—it can be a bundle or a limited edition that you don't expect to sell, but it must be priced strategically to make your target option the obvious choice.

To implement the decoy effect on Shopify, you can create product variants or separate product listings. For example, if you sell t-shirts, you might have a single shirt at $25. Add a two-pack at $45 and a three-pack at $60. The two-pack is the decoy—it's not a great deal compared to the three-pack, but it makes the three-pack look like the best value. Many customers will choose the three-pack, increasing your average order value. The key is to ensure the decoy is priced so that the target option (the one you want to sell) is clearly superior in value.

Practical Applications on Shopify: From Product Pages to Checkout

Now that you understand the principles, let's look at how to apply them directly in your Shopify store. The most common place is the product page, where you can display multiple variants or bundles. Use the compare-at price feature to show a higher anchor, and consider adding a "most popular" badge to your target option to further nudge customers. But don't stop there—your pricing strategy should extend to your entire store architecture.

One effective approach is to create tiered pricing for your products. Instead of offering a single product, create three versions: a basic, a standard, and a premium. The standard should be your main seller, with the premium acting as a decoy to make the standard look like a great deal. For example, if you sell a skincare set, you could have a 30-day supply at $29, a 60-day supply at $49, and a 90-day supply at $69. The 60-day supply is the decoy—it's not much cheaper per day than the 90-day, but it makes the 90-day look like the best value. Many customers will choose the 90-day, increasing your revenue per order.

Another application is in your cart and checkout process. Use order bumps or one-click upsells that present a higher-priced item as an add-on. For instance, after a customer adds a $50 product to their cart, show a popup offering a $75 premium version with additional features. The $50 product now serves as an anchor, making the $75 seem like a reasonable upgrade. You can also use urgency and scarcity to enhance the effect—for example, "Only 3 left at this price" or "Price increases in 24 hours." These tactics work because they combine anchoring with loss aversion, making the customer feel they're getting a deal before it's gone.

Don't forget about your collection pages. When customers browse multiple products, the first product they see can anchor their perception of your store's price level. If you want to position your store as premium, lead with your higher-priced items. If you want to attract budget-conscious shoppers, start with lower prices. But be careful—if you mix too many price points without a clear strategy, you may confuse customers. Instead, use collections to segment products by price range, and within each collection, apply the decoy effect by showing a few higher-priced items alongside your target products.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid When Using Pricing Psychology

While anchoring and decoy effects are powerful, they can backfire if not executed carefully. One common mistake is setting anchors that are too extreme or unrealistic. If you show a $1,000 product next to a $50 product, customers may feel you're trying to trick them, and they'll lose trust. The anchor should be plausible—it should reflect a real market value or a premium version of your product. For example, if you sell handmade jewelry, a $200 anchor for a $50 piece might work, but a $2,000 anchor would be absurd.

Another pitfall is overcomplicating your pricing structure. If you offer too many options, customers may experience choice paralysis and leave without buying. The decoy effect works best with three options—no more, no less. Keep it simple: one target option, one decoy, and one budget option. Also, ensure that the decoy is clearly inferior to the target in terms of value. If the decoy is too similar to the target, customers might choose it, and you'll lose revenue. For instance, if you have a 60-day supply at $49 and a 90-day supply at $69, the 60-day is a decoy because the 90-day offers better value per day. But if the 60-day were priced at $45, it might become the better deal, and customers would choose it, defeating the purpose.

Finally, don't rely solely on pricing psychology to drive sales. It should complement a solid overall marketing strategy. If your product quality is poor or your customer service is lacking, no amount of anchoring will save you. Use these techniques to enhance your existing strengths, not to mask weaknesses. Also, be transparent—never use fake compare-at prices or misleading discounts. In the long run, trust is more valuable than a one-time sale.

Testing and Optimizing Your Pricing Strategy

As with any marketing tactic, you need to test and refine your pricing strategy to see what works best for your audience. Start by implementing one change at a time—for example, add a decoy option to your best-selling product and monitor the conversion rate and average order value over a few weeks. Use A/B testing tools like Shopify's built-in experiments or third-party apps to compare different pricing structures. You might find that a certain anchor price works better for one product category but not another.

Also, consider segmenting your audience. New visitors may respond differently to pricing than returning customers. You can use dynamic pricing apps to show different prices based on user behavior, but be cautious—this can feel manipulative if not done transparently. Instead, focus on creating a consistent pricing strategy that aligns with your brand positioning. If you're a luxury brand, your anchors should be high; if you're a value brand, your anchors should be lower but still create a sense of savings.

Finally, track your metrics beyond just conversion rate. Look at customer lifetime value, repeat purchase rate, and profit margins. A higher average order value might come with lower margins if you're offering discounts. The goal is to maximize overall profitability, not just sales volume. Use analytics to see which pricing strategies lead to the most profitable customers over time.

Conclusion

Pricing is not just a number—it's a powerful psychological tool that can shape customer perception and drive purchasing decisions. By mastering anchoring and the decoy effect, you can guide your Shopify customers toward the options that benefit both them and your bottom line. Start by auditing your current pricing structure. Are you using compare-at prices effectively? Do you have a decoy option on your best sellers? Are you presenting your products in a way that makes your target price seem like a steal? Implement these strategies gradually, test them, and refine based on data. Remember, the goal is not to trick customers but to present your products in a way that highlights their true value. When done right, pricing psychology can be the secret weapon that sets your ecommerce empire apart from the competition.
